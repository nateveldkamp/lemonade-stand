🍋 Lemonade Stand

When Josh Waitzkin teaches someone chess, the first thing he does is take most of the pieces off the board.

Waitzkin was a chess prodigy, the kid the movie Searching for Bobby Fischer is about. He doesn't start beginners with openings and rulebooks and memorized lines. He clears the board down to a king and a pawn against a lone king. Three pieces. On that nearly empty board, a beginner can actually feel how the pieces move and what they want, instead of drowning in the other thirty. Then he adds pieces back a few at a time, until the whole board is there and the student understands it from the inside rather than from a manual.

Playing the whole game

David Perkins, an education researcher at Harvard, has a name for what Waitzkin is doing. In his book Making Learning Whole, he calls it playing the whole game.

Think about how baseball gets taught next to how math gets taught. Nobody hands a seven-year-old a rulebook and says come back in a few years. They set up t-ball, a junior version of the whole game, and the kid plays on day one: hitting, running the bases, the actual thing, just smaller. The finer points get added later, once the kid already cares about the game.

Math, and most of what happens in a classroom, runs the opposite way. Years of drills and definitions first, the actual game (using any of it for something real) maybe never. Perkins has two words for how this goes wrong. Elementitis: learning the elements of something without the story of how they fit together. Abouttitis: learning about a thing without ever doing it.

His fix is the t-ball fix. Invent a junior version of the real game, simple but still recognizably the whole thing, and play it from the very first day.

Why play does the heavy lifting

Part of why t-ball works is that it's play, which is the oldest learning technology there is. A whole generation picked up more about running a business from RollerCoaster Tycoon than from any class. Zoo Tycoon, Mall Tycoon, SimCity, the entire shelf of them quietly taught pricing, cash flow, supply and demand, and the long climb from a small operation to a complicated one. No one lectured those kids on unit economics. They were just trying to keep the lines short and the park out of the red, and the lessons came in through the side door, because the whole thing was a game.

Play lets a person run the entire game cheaply, lose, shrug, and run it again. That loop is where intuition actually comes from.

The simplest whole business

So here's the question this whole project started from. To teach someone the real game of business, not the elements of it, not a class about it, but the actual game, what's the t-ball version?

It would be the simplest thing that's still unmistakably a whole business. Small enough to grasp in an afternoon, complete enough that it's really the entire job: make something, get people to buy it, don't run out, and check whether any money was left at the end. There's an obvious answer, and it happens to be a card table on a sidewalk.

Three questions

The lemonade stand isn't just the American kid's first business by tradition. It was also one of the first business simulations ever put on a computer.

In 1973, a man named Bob Jamison at the Minnesota Educational Computing Consortium wrote a game called Lemonade Stand for the room-sized timeshare machines that schools dialed into. Six years later, in February 1979, an Apple engineer named Charlie Kellner ported it to the Apple II, gave it color and a little soundtrack (when a thunderstorm wiped out the day's sales, the computer played a few bars of "Singin' in the Rain"), and Apple started bundling it with the machine. So for a lot of families, the very first program that ever ran on their very first computer was a tiny business.

Every morning the game shows the weather and then asks three questions:

  • How many glasses of lemonade to make?
  • How many signs to put up?
  • What price to charge?

That's the whole game. Three numbers a day.

And here's the part worth slowing down for. Those three questions are the three chess pieces.

Nearly every decision a business ever makes, all the way up to a global company with factories and a stock ticker, is a more elaborate version of one of those three. How many glasses to make is the question of matching supply to demand, and how much money to put at risk to do it. How many signs is the question of how to create demand. What price is the question of how to capture the value once it's been created. Take any giant company, clear the board down to almost nothing, and what's left is a card table, a pitcher, and three questions.

That's the junior version. You can play the whole game of business on it, then add the pieces back.

The new piece on the board

There's one piece on the board today that Bob Jamison and Charlie Kellner never had: an AI sitting right next to you.

That changes things enough that it's worth learning the two together instead of apart. Most training treats AI as a side subject, a tool to bolt on after the real work is already understood. But the work doesn't happen that way anymore. The business thinking and the AI are one motion now: decide what to do, and do it with an AI in the loop. So this course teaches them as a single skill, by growing a lemonade stand from that card table into a company, with an AI as the partner the whole way up.

And it teaches them the way anything worth learning gets learned. By playing. Starting right now.

Where this goes

That little game is the whole course in miniature. You just ran a real business for a day, with an AI, and every number you picked was a door into a real skill: pricing, marketing, operations, finance, reading risk. The stand grows from here one piece at a time, and what you can do with it grows the same way.

Here's why it's worth the climb. AI reshaped software engineering faster than any other kind of work, not because coders are special, but because their work already lives in one organized, version-tracked place an AI can fully see and work inside. Most knowledge work isn't there yet. The context is scattered across inboxes, slide decks, and spreadsheets.
